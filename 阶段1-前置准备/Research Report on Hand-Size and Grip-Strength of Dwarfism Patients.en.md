# Research Report on the Hand-Size and Grip-Strength Characteristics of Patients with Dwarfism

## 1 Introduction

Dwarfism is a class of inherited skeletal disorders causing markedly short stature, of which the most common type is **achondroplasia**, accounting for more than 70 % of all dwarfism cases[^1]. Achondroplasia is caused by a mutation in the FGFR3 gene, and its pathological feature is mainly impaired endochondral ossification of the long bones (especially the humerus and femur), producing **rhizomelic shortening**—that is, the upper arm and thigh are shortened most markedly while the trunk length is relatively close to that of a normal person[^2].

In the custom design of a table tennis racket, hand size and grip strength are the core input variables that determine the handle geometry and the racket weight. This report systematically reviews the hand-size, grip-strength, and related anthropometric data of patients with achondroplasia, providing a data basis for the second task of Phase 1: "understand the hand-size ranges of patients with dwarfism (palm width, grip strength, etc.)".

## 2 Overall Body-Size Parameters of Patients with Achondroplasia

### 2.1 Height and Weight

The CLARITY natural-history study (n = 1,341) provides the largest sample of anthropometric data on patients with achondroplasia to date. The adult final-height data come from 277 individuals (128 males, 149 females), and the results are shown in Table 1[^3].

| Sex | Mean height (cm) | Standard deviation (cm) | Imperial equivalent |
| :--- | :----------: | :--------: | :------: |
| Male | 129.9 | 6.25 | 4′3.1″ |
| Female | 122.4 | 5.90 | 4′0.2″ |

> **Table 1: Mean adult height of patients with achondroplasia**

In a Norwegian population-based study (n = 49), the mean height of patients with achondroplasia was 135.4 cm (males) and 129.7 cm (females)[^4]. The difference from the CLARITY data may arise from differences in region and population composition.

### 2.2 Body-Proportion Characteristics

A Swedish–German collaborative study (n ≈ 450) reported in detail the developmental trajectory of body proportions in children with achondroplasia[^5]. In adulthood, the key proportional parameters are as follows:

| Measurement item | Male (cm) | Female (cm) | Reference value for the general population (male/female) |
| :--- | :-----: | :-----: | :------------: |
| Sitting height | 86.1 | 85.0 | 90.7 / — |
| Arm span | 122 | 110 | 186 / 174 |
| Leg length | 44 | 40 | 88 / 80 (estimated) |
| Foot length | approx. 18–20 | approx. 17–19 | 25–28 / 22–25 |

> **Table 2: Body-proportion parameters of adults with achondroplasia (based on Merker et al., 2018)**

Key finding: **the sitting height is only about 5–7 cm lower than that of a normal person, but the arm span is about 60 cm shorter and the leg length about 40 cm shorter**. This means that the total upper-limb length is markedly shortened, and the fingers and palm are correspondingly smaller. The sitting-height/height ratio is about 66 %–67 %, far higher than the 51 %–53 % of a normal person, reflecting the characteristic of extremely shortened lower limbs with a relatively normal trunk[^5].

## 3 Hand-Size Characteristics

### 3.1 Qualitative Description

The hands of patients with achondroplasia have the following typical morphological features[^1][^2]:

- **Short and broad hands**: both the metacarpals and the phalanges are markedly shortened, and the palm is wide and thick.
- **Trident hand**: the middle and ring fingers cannot be fully brought together, and the 3rd and 4th fingers separate in a V shape; this is a hallmark feature in the neonatal period.
- **Brachydactyly**: all fingers are shortened and the interphalangeal joints are enlarged.
- **Limited thumb mobility**: because of the shortened carpal and metacarpal bones, thumb opposition may be limited.

### 3.2 Segmental Length of the Upper Limb

A study of Egyptian patients with achondroplasia (n = 20) performed anthropometric measurements on each upper-limb segment; all measured values were below the 3rd percentile of normal peers, with **the upper arm (humerus) being the most markedly shortened**, quantitatively supporting the characteristic of rhizomelia[^6].

The total upper-limb length, upper-arm length, forearm length, and **hand length** are all affected. Although that study did not report the specific value of hand length separately, it explicitly stated that **all hand-related dimensions were below the 3rd percentile**.

### 3.3 Hand-Size Reference for the General Population

To establish a comparison baseline, Table 3 lists the main hand-size data from the Chinese national standard for adult body dimensions (GB/T 10000-2023)[^7].

| Measurement item | Male P50 (mm) | Female P50 (mm) | Male P5 (mm) | Female P5 (mm) |
| :------- | :----------: | :----------: | :---------: | :---------: |
| Hand length | 183 | 171 | 173 | 159 |
| Hand width | 82 | 76 | 77 | 70 |
| Index-finger length | 69 | 66 | 64 | 61 |
| Palm circumference | — | — | — | — |

> **Table 3: Percentiles of Chinese adult hand dimensions (GB/T 10000-2023)**

It is noteworthy that **the hand size of patients with achondroplasia is expected to be markedly below the 5th percentile of the above normal population**. Combining this with height-proportion estimation, the hand length of an adult male with achondroplasia is approximately **13–15 cm** and the hand width approximately **6–8 cm**. This is consistent with the clinical rule of thumb that patients with dwarfism have a "palm width < 7 cm".

### 3.4 Summary of Hand-Size Data

Based on a comprehensive inference from the literature, the hand-size ranges of patients with achondroplasia are estimated as follows:

| Parameter | Adult male | Adult female | Basis of estimate |
| :------------- | :------: | :------: | :--------------------------------- |
| Hand length (cm) | 13–15 | 12–14 | Estimated from height proportions; < P5 of the normal population |
| Palm width (cm) | 6.5–8.0 | 6.0–7.5 | Hand width scales down with height + broad-hand feature |
| Palm length (cm) | 7–9 | 6–8 | About 55 %–60 % of hand length |
| Thumb length (cm) | 4–5 | 3.5–4.5 | Brachydactyly |
| Middle-finger length (cm) | 5–6.5 | 4.5–5.5 | Finger length about 60 %–75 % of normal |
| Palm circumference (cm) | 16–19 | 15–18 | Palm width × proportional coefficient |

> **Table 4: Estimated hand-size ranges of patients with achondroplasia**

| 60–69 | 32 | 20 | 20 | 12 |
| 70–80 | 27 | 17 | 16 | 10 |

> **Table 5: Percentiles of grip strength of Chinese adults (CNHS, n = 39,655)**

### 4.2 Grip-Strength Data of Patients with Achondroplasia

A Norwegian study of adults with achondroplasia (n = 43) used the Grippit electronic grip-strength dynamometer to test grip strength and compared the results with age- and sex-matched reference values for the Norwegian general population[^9]. The results showed:

- There was **no significant difference** between the **maximum and mean grip strength** of male patients with achondroplasia and their matched controls.
- The results for female patients were similar, with grip-strength levels comparable to those of the controls.
- However, the Grippit device used in the study requires consideration of the influence of hand-size fit on the measurement results.

This finding suggests that: **despite their smaller hand size, the absolute strength of the hand muscles of patients with achondroplasia may not be proportionally reduced**. This may be related to the need for patients to rely more on upper-limb strength to compensate for lower-limb dysfunction in daily activities.

### 4.3 Association between Grip Strength and Physical Fitness

The same study also reported that the patients' **cardiorespiratory fitness (VO₂peak = 28.4 mL/kg/min, females) was significantly lower than that of the controls (36.3)**, that the **6-minute walk test (6MWT) distance was significantly shorter (females 447 m vs. 644 m)**, and that the number of repetitions in the **30-second sit-to-stand test (30sSTS) was lower (females 23.7 vs. 24.8, n.s.)**[^10].

These data indicate that the patients' overall physical-fitness level is low, which may indirectly affect swing endurance. However, **in terms of hand grip strength, the patients display relatively preserved functional capacity**.

## 5 Implications for Table Tennis Racket Design

### 5.1 Handle-Design Parameters

Based on the above data analysis, the following design recommendations are proposed:

| Design parameter | Recommended range | Basis |
| :----------- | :------------ | :---------------------------------------- |
| Handle circumference | 65–80 mm | Corresponds to a palm width < 7 cm; a 15 %–25 % reduction relative to a standard racket (85–95 mm) |
| Handle length | 60–75 mm | Palm length about 7–9 cm; shortened relative to a standard racket (80–85 mm) |
| Handle cross-section | Elliptical / octagonal | The fingers are short, so an excessively thick cross-section must be avoided to prevent an unstable grip |
| Handle material | Cork / sweat-absorbing band | Grip strength may be low, so the friction coefficient should be increased to prevent slippage |

### 5.2 Racket-Weight Design

| Design parameter | Recommended range | Basis |
| :----------- | :--------- | :----------------------------------------------------- |
| Target total weight | 110–130 g | A 30 %–40 % weight reduction relative to a standard racket (170–200 g), lowering the joint load |
| Balance point | Biased towards the handle | Short arm span → amplified torque effect → reduce the moment of inertia by shifting the CG rearward |
| Handle counterweight | Adjustable | Adapts to different grip-strength levels and allows individual adjustment |

### 5.3 Points to Note

- **Large individual variation**: the hand size of patients with achondroplasia varies considerably; an **adjustable or modular** handle scheme is recommended.
- **Preserved grip strength**: studies indicate that grip strength may be relatively preserved, so the need for weight reduction may derive more from **joint protection** than from an absolute lack of strength.
- **Arm-length factor**: the arm span is only about 110–122 cm (versus 170–190 cm in a normal person); the shorter moment arm requires a markedly lower racket moment of inertia.

---

## 6 Conclusion

This report synthesises the anthropometric and grip-strength data from several studies of patients with achondroplasia, providing key parameter bases for the custom design of a table tennis racket:

1. **The estimated palm-width range is 6.0–8.0 cm**, markedly below the 5th percentile of the normal population (7.7 cm for males, 7.0 cm for females), requiring the handle circumference to be reduced to **65–80 mm**.
2. **Grip strength may be relatively preserved**, but overall physical fitness (cardiorespiratory endurance, lower-limb strength) is low; weight reduction and joint protection remain core requirements.
3. **The arm span is only 110–122 cm**, about 60 cm shorter than that of the normal population, requiring a marked rearward shift of the racket's CG to reduce the moment of inertia.
4. Given the large individual variation among patients, an **adjustable handle and counterweight scheme** is recommended to meet individual needs.
