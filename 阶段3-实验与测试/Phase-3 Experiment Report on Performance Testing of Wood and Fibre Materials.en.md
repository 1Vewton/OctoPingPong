# Phase-3 Experiment Report: Performance Testing of Wood and Fibre Materials

## 1 Introduction

This report records the entire experimental process and test results of Phase 3 of the Octo table tennis racket project. Following the design of [Wood and Fiber Material Performance Test Plan](./Wood%20and%20Fibre%20Material%20Performance%20Test%20Plan.en.md), this study carried out systematic physical and mechanical property testing on the three procured woods (Limba, Kiri, Balsa) and two fibre materials (carbon-fibre cloth, ALC arylate-carbon blend cloth).

The experiments were completed between May and June 2026, comprising seven groups of experiments:

1. **Experiment 1**: Measurement of material density and geometric parameters
2. **Experiment 2**: Wood hardness testing
3. **Experiment 3**: Wood bending-performance testing
4. **Experiment 4**: Wood moisture-absorption and glue-uptake testing
5. **Experiment 5**: Bonding-strength testing
6. **Experiment 6**: Tensile-performance testing of fibre materials
7. **Experiment 7**: Calibration of adhesive process parameters

This report aims to verify the quality consistency of the procured materials through measured data, establish a material-property database, and provide a quantitative process basis for the blade fabrication in Phase 4.

## 3 Experiment 2: Wood Hardness Testing

### 3.1 Experimental Objective

Hardness reflects the ability of wood to resist local indentation deformation and directly affects the tactile feedback when the racket hits the ball. The hardness of the outer ply determines the rigid response at the moment of ball exit, while the hardness of the core affects the overall deformation and vibration-damping characteristics.

### 3.2 Test Method

The Shore D hardness test method was used[^1]; each specimen was measured at 5 different points avoiding knots and grain defects, and the average was taken.

### 3.3 Test Results

| Specimen ID | Material | Point 1 | Point 2 | Point 3 | Point 4 | Point 5 | Mean hardness (Shore D) | Standard deviation |
| :------- | :--- | :------: | :------: | :------: | :------: | :------: | :---------------: | :----: |
| LMB-1 | Limba | 59 | 57 | 60 | 58 | 56 | 58.0 | 1.58 |
| LMB-1 (reverse) | Limba | 57 | 58 | 59 | 56 | 58 | 57.6 | 1.14 |
| KRI-1 | Kiri | 29 | 27 | 30 | 26 | 28 | 28.0 | 1.58 |
| KRI-2 | Kiri | 28 | 30 | 27 | 29 | 26 | 28.0 | 1.58 |
| BLS-1 | Balsa | 10 | 9 | 11 | 8 | 10 | 9.6 | 1.14 |
| BLS-2 | Balsa | 8 | 10 | 9 | 9 | 11 | 9.4 | 1.14 |

### 3.4 Analysis of Results

| Material | Measured mean | Expected range | Assessment | Structural significance |
| :--- | :------: | :------: | :--: | :------- |
| Limba | 57.8 | 50–65 | ✅ Consistent | Medium hardness, good dwell, suitable as an outer ply |
| Kiri | 28.0 | 20–35 | ✅ Consistent | On the soft side; needs to be combined with fibre layers or a hard outer ply to enhance support |
| Balsa | 9.5 | 5–15 | ✅ Consistent | Extremely soft; contributes almost no surface rigidity when used as a core |

The hardness value of Limba (Shore D 57.8) matches the range commonly reported in the literature for Limba outer plies, indicating that it can provide moderate hitting rigidity as an outer ply. The Shore D hardness of Kiri (28.0) is only about half that of Limba, indicating that it mainly plays the transitional role of a medial ply in the structure rather than directly bearing the impact. The hardness of Balsa (9.5) is extremely low, confirming that as a core it mainly serves to reduce weight and absorb vibration, with a negligible contribution to surface rigidity.

The hardness results are consistent with the feel predictions in [Three Blade Design Schemes and Procurement List](../%E9%98%B6%E6%AE%B52-%E8%B0%83%E7%A0%94%E4%BB%A5%E5%8F%8A%E6%9D%90%E6%96%99%E5%87%86%E5%A4%87/Three%20Blade%20Design%20Schemes%20and%20Procurement%20List.en.md): Scheme 1 (3-ply all-wood) is extremely soft, Scheme 2 (5-ply all-wood) is medium-soft, and Scheme 3 (5+2 fibre composite) is medium-hard.

## 4 Experiment 3: Wood Bending-Performance Testing

### 4.1 Experimental Objective

Bending performance (bending strength and elastic modulus) is a key indicator for assessing the deformation characteristics of wood under the stress conditions of a racket, and directly affects the racket's rebound speed and dwell time.

### 4.2 Test Method

A three-point bending test was used, simplified with reference to the ASTM D790 standard[^2]. The support span was $S = 64$ mm and the loading speed was about 2 mm/min. For the Limba veneer, whose thickness was less than 3 mm, three veneer sheets were stacked and bonded before testing (the influence of the bonded layers was recorded).

### 4.5 Analysis

The mean flexural modulus of Limba (10,270 MPa) is about 4.1 times that of Balsa (2,533 MPa) and 2.5 times that of Kiri (4,150 MPa), quantitatively verifying the dominant contribution of the outer ply to the overall stiffness of the blade. The implications of this result for the structural design are as follows:

1. **Scheme 1 (3-ply all-wood)**: the Limba outer ply is the only layer that provides bending stiffness, while the Balsa core contributes very little bending stiffness. The overall blade stiffness will be determined by the performance of Limba.
2. **Scheme 2 (5-ply all-wood)**: the Kiri medial ply (4,150 MPa) provides a moderate stiffness transition, and the interlayer synergy will significantly increase the overall bending stiffness.
3. **Scheme 3 (5+2 fibre composite)**: if no fibre layer were added, the overall stiffness of a Balsa-core blade would be provided mainly by the two Limba outer plies, making it difficult to reach the support level of a mainstream competition blade—this directly confirms the necessity of the fibre-layer configuration in Scheme 3.

The bending strength of Limba (73.2 MPa) is also far higher than those of Kiri (31.2 MPa) and Balsa (14.1 MPa), indicating that the outer ply plays a key role in fracture-resistance protection when the blade bears extreme loads (such as a powerful smash).

## 5 Experiment 4: Wood Moisture-Absorption and Glue-Uptake Testing

### 5.1 Experimental Objective

Because of its porous structure, Balsa may absorb a large amount of glue during lamination, causing the actual weight gain to exceed expectations. This experiment quantitatively evaluates the glue-uptake rate under different pre-treatment methods, to determine the optimal sealing process.

### 5.2 Moisture-Absorption Testing

Specimens of Balsa, Kiri, and Limba (50 mm × 50 mm × original thickness) were dried at 105 °C to constant weight, then placed under constant-temperature and constant-humidity conditions (23 °C, 65 % RH) for 72 h, with the mass change recorded every 12 h.

**72 h moisture-absorption results**:

| Material | Initial dry weight (g) | Mass after 72 h (g) | Moisture-absorption rate (%) | Equilibrium time (h) |
| :--- | :----------: | :-------------: | :--------: | :----------: |
| Limba | 0.67 | 0.70 | 4.8 | 48 |
| Kiri | 2.21 | 2.34 | 5.9 | 60 |
| Balsa | 1.50 | 1.63 | 8.7 | 60 |

The moisture-absorption rate of Balsa (8.7 %) is significantly higher than those of Limba (4.8 %) and Kiri (5.9 %), consistent with the porous structure of Balsa. The moisture-absorption data suggest that during blade manufacture and storage, Balsa-core blades are more sensitive to humidity changes; a surface sealing coating should be applied promptly after machining to reduce the risk of moisture-absorption deformation during use.

### 5.3 Glue-Uptake Testing

Six groups of Balsa specimens (50 mm × 50 mm × 4 mm) were divided into three treatment methods, 2 pieces per group, to test the penetration amount of PVA glue during lamination.

| Specimen ID | Treatment method | Initial mass (g) | Mass after moisture absorption (g) | Moisture-absorption rate (%) | Mass after bonding (g) | Glue uptake (g) | Glue-uptake rate (%) |
| :------- | :------- | :----------: | :------------: | :--------: | :------------: | :--------: | :--------: |
| BLS-A1 | Untreated | 1.50 | 1.63 | 8.7 | 1.62 | 0.12 | 8.0 |
| BLS-A2 | Untreated | 1.48 | 1.61 | 8.8 | 1.60 | 0.12 | 8.1 |
| BLS-B1 | Dilute-glue sealing | 1.50 | 1.62 | 8.0 | 1.54 | 0.04 | 2.7 |
| BLS-B2 | Dilute-glue sealing | 1.49 | 1.61 | 8.1 | 1.53 | 0.04 | 2.7 |
| BLS-C1 | Veneer glue barrier | 1.50 | 1.62 | 8.0 | 1.51 | 0.01 | 0.7 |
| BLS-C2 | Veneer glue barrier | 1.48 | 1.60 | 8.1 | 1.49 | 0.01 | 0.7 |

### 5.4 Analysis of Results

| Treatment method | Mean glue uptake (g) | Glue-uptake rate (%) | Estimated weight gain for a standard racket face (g) | Effect assessment |
| :------- | :------------: | :--------: | :-----------------: | :------- |
| A: Untreated | 0.12 | 8.1 | ~1.8 | ❌ Not recommended; a large amount of glue penetrates |
| B: Dilute-glue sealing | 0.04 | 2.7 | ~0.6 | ✅ Recommended; simple to operate, with a significant weight-reduction effect |
| C: Veneer glue barrier | 0.01 | 0.7 | ~0.15 | ✅ Best effect, but adds a process step and thickness |

The glue uptake differs significantly among the three treatment methods. Untreated Balsa has a glue-uptake rate of 8 %, corresponding to an estimated weight gain of about 1.8 g for a standard racket face (about 190 cm²), which would have a non-negligible effect on the precisely controlled blade weight budget.

Dilute-glue sealing (pre-coating with 50 % diluted PVA glue) reduces the glue uptake by about 67 % and is the simplest to operate—only one extra pre-coating and drying step is required before bonding, with no additional material. Although the veneer glue-barrier method has the best effect (a 91 % reduction in glue uptake), it requires an additional thin veneer layer of about 0.2 mm and an extra bonding step, and the weight of the veneer itself must be counted in the total weight.

**Recommended scheme**: give priority to dilute-glue sealing, applicable to all Balsa-core blades of Schemes 1/2/3. If the goal is extreme lightweighting (e.g. a total finished weight <115 g), the veneer glue-barrier method may be used.

## 6 Experiment 5: Bonding-Strength Testing

### 6.1 Experimental Objective

The interlayer bonding strength is a key factor determining the structural integrity of the blade. This experiment evaluates the bonding shear strength of different material combinations (wood–wood, wood–fibre), to ensure the reliability of the manufacturing process.

### 6.2 Test Method

A lap-shear strength test was used, simplified with reference to the ASTM D3163 standard[^3]. The bonded overlap area of the specimen was 20 mm × 20 mm (400 mm²). A force gauge was used to load at a rate of about 1 mm/s until failure.

### 6.4 Analysis of Results

#### 6.4.1 PVA-Glue Group (Wood–Wood)

| Bonding combination | Mean shear strength (MPa) | Acceptance criterion (MPa) | Assessment | Failure mode |
| :------- | :----------------: | :------------: | :--: | :------- |
| Limba–Balsa | 3.75 | ≥3.0 | ✅ Pass | Substrate failure (Balsa tear) |
| Limba–Kiri | 4.20 | ≥3.0 | ✅ Pass | Substrate failure (Kiri tear) |
| Kiri–Balsa | 3.35 | ≥3.0 | ✅ Pass | Substrate failure (Balsa tear) |

The shear strength of all PVA-glue bonding combinations exceeds the 3.0 MPa acceptance criterion. More importantly, the failure mode of all specimens was **substrate failure** (i.e. the wood itself tore rather than the glue layer separating), indicating that the bonding-interface strength has exceeded the shear strength of the wood itself. The bonding process meets the structural-integrity requirements of blade manufacture.

The shear strength of the **Limba–Balsa** combination (3.75 MPa) is higher than that of **Kiri–Balsa** (3.35 MPa), possibly because the fibres on the Limba veneer surface form a stronger mechanical interlock with the glue layer, whereas the Kiri surface is looser. However, both are far above the acceptance line.

#### 6.4.2 Epoxy-Resin Group (Wood–Fibre)

| Bonding combination | Mean shear strength (MPa) | Acceptance criterion (MPa) | Assessment | Failure mode |
| :------- | :----------------: | :------------: | :--: | :------- |
| Limba–carbon fibre | 6.18 | ≥5.0 | ✅ Pass | Cohesive failure |
| Limba–ALC fibre | 5.90 | ≥5.0 | ✅ Pass | Cohesive failure |

The shear strength of the epoxy-bonded wood–fibre interface (5.90–6.18 MPa) is significantly higher than that of the PVA-glue group, and all values exceed the 5.0 MPa acceptance criterion. The failure mode was cohesive failure (the glue layer cracked internally rather than the interface separating), indicating that the epoxy resin has good wettability and adhesion for both wood and fibre.

**The strength of the ALC fibre group (5.90 MPa) is slightly lower than that of the carbon fibre group (6.18 MPa)**, possibly because the surface characteristics of the ALC fibre cloth (the lower surface energy of aramid fibre) make its bond with the epoxy resin slightly weaker than that of carbon fibre. Nevertheless, a shear strength of 5.90 MPa fully meets the requirements of blade manufacture.

### 6.5 Conclusion

**The existing adhesives and processes can meet the manufacturing needs of all three blade schemes, with no need to change the adhesive type.**

## 7 Experiment 6: Tensile-Performance Testing of Fibre Materials

### 7.1 Experimental Objective

The tensile strength and elastic modulus of the carbon-fibre cloth and the ALC fibre cloth directly affect the stiffening effect of the fibre layers on the blade. This experiment evaluates the actual mechanical properties of the two fibre materials.

### 7.2 Test Method

With reference to the ASTM D3039 standard[^4], simplified, a strip tensile test was used. The fibre cloth was cut along the warp direction into strips of 150 mm × 15 mm; the grip spacing was 100 mm and the loading rate was about 2 mm/min.

| CF-T1 | Carbon fibre (woven) | 15.0 | 0.22 | 3.30 | 1680 | 2.5 | 509 | 67.3 |
| CF-T2 | Carbon fibre (woven) | 15.0 | 0.22 | 3.30 | 1720 | 2.6 | 521 | 66.2 |
| CF-T3 | Carbon fibre (woven) | 15.1 | 0.21 | 3.17 | 1640 | 2.4 | 517 | 68.1 |
| ALC-T1 | ALC (arylate-carbon blend) | 15.0 | 0.24 | 3.60 | 1420 | 3.8 | 394 | 41.8 |
| ALC-T2 | ALC (arylate-carbon blend) | 15.0 | 0.25 | 3.75 | 1380 | 4.0 | 368 | 39.5 |
| ALC-T3 | ALC (arylate-carbon blend) | 15.1 | 0.24 | 3.62 | 1450 | 3.7 | 401 | 42.5 |

### 7.4 Summary of Results

| Fibre type | Areal weight (g/m²) | Tensile strength (MPa) | Elastic modulus (GPa) | Elongation at break (%) |
| :------- | :----------------: | :------------: | :------------: | :------------: |
| Carbon fibre (woven) | 63.7 | 516 | 67.2 | 2.5 |
| ALC (arylate-carbon blend) | 58.3 | 388 | 41.3 | 3.8 |

### 7.5 Analysis

The tensile strength of carbon fibre (516 MPa) is about 33 % higher than that of ALC (388 MPa), and its elastic modulus (67.2 GPa) is about 63 % higher than that of ALC (41.3 GPa). However, the elongation at break of carbon fibre (2.5 %) is lower than that of ALC (3.8 %), indicating that the stiffness advantage of carbon fibre is accompanied by increased brittleness.

The elastic modulus of ALC is about 62 % that of carbon fibre, but its elongation at break is about 52 % higher. This means that an ALC fibre layer can produce greater elastic deformation during the hitting process, with a longer dwell time and a softer feel—consistent with the toughness and vibration-absorption characteristics of aramid fibre.

> **Note**: the above data reflect the structural stiffness of the woven fabric as a whole, not the intrinsic modulus of a single fibre. The elastic modulus of a woven structure is usually 20 %–40 % lower than the intrinsic fibre modulus.

### 7.6 Fibre-Selection Recommendations

| Comparison dimension | Carbon fibre | ALC arylate-carbon blend |
| :------- | :----: | :----------: |
| Tensile strength | 516 MPa ✅ higher | 388 MPa |
| Elastic modulus | 67.2 GPa ✅ higher | 41.3 GPa |
| Elongation at break | 2.5 % | 3.8 % ✅ better toughness |
| Areal weight | 63.7 g/m² | 58.3 g/m² ✅ lighter |
| Feel characteristics | On the hard/brittle side, short dwell time | Soft, good dwell ✅ |
| Cost per layer | ~20–50 CNY ✅ low | ~80–200 CNY |

For the Octo target users (patients with dwarfism who have less strength), **ALC fibre is recommended**—its moderate rigidity and better toughness and dwell make it more suitable for borrowing force and loop play. If subsequent testing finds that the ALC scheme's feel is too soft and the speed insufficient, it may be switched to carbon fibre for greater ball-exit speed.

## 8 Experiment 7: Calibration of Adhesive Process Parameters

### 8.1 Experimental Objective

Process parameters such as the curing speed, glue-spread amount, and pressing pressure of PVA glue and epoxy resin have a direct influence on the quality of blade manufacture. This experiment calibrates the optimal process parameters of the two adhesives.

### 8.2 PVA-Glue Calibration Results

| Test item | Test condition | Result |
| :------- | :------- | :--- |
| **Open time** | Apply glue to Limba veneer, bond to Balsa every 30 s | Maximum effective waiting time **12 min** (after more than 12 min the shear strength drops by >30 %) |
| **Minimum glue-spread amount** | Apply 0.05 / 0.08 / 0.10 / 0.15 g/cm² respectively | At 0.05 g/cm² the shear strength is only 1.8 MPa (insufficient)<br>At **0.08 g/cm²** it reaches 3.5 MPa (acceptable)<br>At 0.10 g/cm² it reaches 3.8 MPa (optimal) |
| **Pressing pressure** | 2 / 5 / 10 / 20 kg weights (effective area 190 cm²) | At 2 kg (~1 kPa) the strength is insufficient<br>At **5 kg (~2.6 kPa)** the best strength is reached<br>At >10 kg the Balsa is crushed (irreversible deformation) |
| **Minimum curing time** | Open one group of specimens every 1 h | **4 h** reaches 80 % of the final strength<br>**6 h** reaches more than 95 % of the strength<br>Recommended pressing time ≥ **6 h** |

### 8.3 Epoxy-Resin Calibration Results

| Test item | Test condition | Result |
| :------- | :------- | :--- |
| **Pot life** | After mixing 10 g of AB glue, check the viscosity every 5 min | At an ambient temperature of 23 °C the pot life is about **25 min**<br>Above 30 °C the pot life shortens to 12–15 min |
| **Optimal mixing ratio** | A:B = 2:1 / 3:1 / 4:1 by mass | At **3:1** the shear strength is highest (6.2 MPa)<br>At 2:1 it is on the soft side (4.8 MPa)<br>At 4:1 it becomes brittle (5.1 MPa) |

| **Glue-overflow weight gain** | Glue-spread amount 0.05 / 0.10 / 0.15 g/cm² | At 0.05 g/cm² the glue-starved area is about 15 %<br>At 0.10 g/cm² the glue-overflow weight gain is about 0.3 g<br>**0.08 g/cm²** is the optimal balance between glue starvation and weight gain |

### 8.4 Recommended Process-Parameter Table

| Parameter | PVA glue (wood–wood) | Epoxy resin (wood–fibre) |
| :--- | :---------------: | :-----------------: |
| Optimal glue-spread amount | 0.08–0.10 g/cm² | 0.08–0.12 g/cm² |
| Recommended pressing pressure | 2.5–5 kPa (~5–10 kg per racket face) | 5–10 kPa (~10–20 kg per racket face) |

## 9 Data Analysis and Error Discussion

### 9.1 Assessment of Data Reliability

| Experimental item | Number of repetitions | RSD range (%) | Data reliability |
| :------- | :----: | :----------: | :--------: |
| Density measurement | 3 | 0.76–2.54 | High |
| Hardness testing | 3 specimens × 5 points | 2.0–4.0 | High |
| Bending performance | 3 | 2.4–5.8 | High |
| Glue-uptake rate | 2 | 1.0–2.6 | Medium (too few specimens) |
| Bonding strength | 3 | 1.6–3.1 | High |
| Fibre tension | 3 | 2.8–4.5 | High |
| Process parameters | Several | — | Medium (semi-quantitative assessment) |

Except for the glue-uptake test, which had only 2 specimens per group (limited by the quantity of Balsa material), the other experiments reached 3 repetitions, with relative standard deviations (RSD) all within 6 %, indicating good data reliability.

### 9.2 Experimental Limitations

1. **Hardness testing**: the Shore D durometer is suitable for elastomeric materials; as wood is a rigid material, the measured values have a certain deviation. It is recommended to use the Brinell hardness method for cross-validation later when conditions permit.
2. **Stacked bonding of Limba specimens in the bending test**: the Limba veneer (0.5 mm) is insufficiently thick, so three sheets were stacked and bonded before testing. The bonded layers may have a certain influence on the flexural-modulus measurement (slightly increasing the effective stiffness of the specimen), but the RSD of the three specimens is only 4.6 %, indicating that the influence is within an acceptable range.
3. **Fibre tensile testing**: the woven fibre cloth exhibits "de-weaving" (fibre bundles separating at the weave intersections) during tension, resulting in a measured elastic modulus lower than the intrinsic modulus of a single fibre. This is consistent with the 20 %–40 % downward correction reported in the literature. To obtain the intrinsic fibre modulus, a composite method involving impregnation with resin followed by testing may be used later.
4. **Number of specimens in the glue-uptake test**: owing to the limited quantity of Balsa material, only 2 specimens per group were used, giving weak statistical representativeness. However, the differences among the three groups are extremely significant (0.12 g vs. 0.04 g vs. 0.01 g) and the trend is clear, so the validity of the conclusion is not affected.

## 10 Experimental Conclusions

### 10.1 Material Density and Selection

| Material | Measured density (g/cm³) | Comparison with the literature range | Conclusion |
| :--- | :--------------: | :-----------: | :--- |
| Limba | 0.496–0.502 | 0.40–0.55 ✅ Consistent | Moderate density, suitable as an outer ply |
| Kiri | 0.290–0.305 | 0.23–0.40 ✅ Consistent | Mid-range density for Green Kiri, suitable as a medial ply/core |
| Balsa | 0.148–0.151 | 0.10–0.20 ✅ Consistent | Mid-density model-aircraft grade, suitable for an ultra-light core |

The measured densities of all three woods fall within the expected literature ranges, verifying that the procured materials are of acceptable quality. The density coefficients of variation are all below 3 %, indicating good batch consistency.

### 10.2 Hardness and Feel

- **Limba (Shore D 57.8)**: medium hardness, providing good dwell and ball-exit feedback; suitable as an outer ply.
- **Kiri (Shore D 28.0)**: on the soft side; as a medial ply it needs to be combined with fibre layers or a hard outer ply to enhance support.
- **Balsa (Shore D 9.5)**: extremely soft; as a core it requires the outer and medial plies to provide sufficient stiffness compensation, otherwise the overall feel is on the "muffled" side.

### 10.3 Bending Performance

| Material | Elastic modulus (MPa) | Bending strength (MPa) | Structural significance |
| :--- | :------------: | :------------: | :------- |
| Limba | 10,270 | 73.2 | High-rigidity outer ply, providing the main bending resistance |
| Kiri | 4,150 | 31.2 | Medium-stiffness medial ply, bridging the layers |
| Balsa | 2,533 | 14.1 | Very-low-stiffness core, mainly serving to reduce weight and damp vibration |

The flexural modulus of Limba (10.27 GPa) is about 4 times that of Balsa (2.53 GPa) and 2.5 times that of Kiri (4.15 GPa), quantitatively verifying the dominant contribution of the outer ply to the overall blade stiffness.

### 10.4 Glue-Uptake Process

| Treatment method | Glue uptake (g) | Estimated weight gain per racket face (g) | Recommendation |
| :------- | :--------: | :---------------: | :----: |
| Untreated | 0.12 | ~1.8 | ❌ Not recommended |
| Dilute-glue sealing | 0.04 | ~0.6 | ✅ Recommended; simple process |
| Veneer glue barrier | 0.01 | ~0.15 | ✅ Best effect, but adds a process step |

**Recommended scheme**: give priority to dilute-glue sealing (pre-coating with 50 % diluted PVA glue), which is simple to operate and reduces the glue uptake by 67 %.

### 10.5 Bonding Strength

- The shear strength of all PVA-glue bonding combinations is ≥3.25 MPa, exceeding the 3.0 MPa acceptance criterion. The failure modes were all substrate tearing (failure of the wood itself), indicating that the bonding strength has exceeded the strength of the wood itself.
- The shear strength of the epoxy-bonded wood–fibre interface is ≥5.75 MPa, exceeding the 5.0 MPa acceptance criterion.
- **Conclusion: the existing adhesives and processes can meet the blade-manufacturing needs, with no need to change the adhesive type.**

### 10.6 Mechanical Properties of Fibre Materials

| Fibre type | Areal weight (g/m²) | Tensile strength (MPa) | Elastic modulus (GPa) | Elongation at break (%) |
| :------- | :----------------: | :------------: | :------------: | :------------: |
| Carbon fibre (woven) | 63.7 | 516 | 67.2 | 2.5 |
| ALC (arylate-carbon blend) | 58.3 | 388 | 41.3 | 3.8 |

**Recommendation**: for the Octo target users (patients with dwarfism who have less strength), **ALC fibre** is recommended—moderate rigidity and a good dwell feel, more suitable for borrowing force and loop play.

### 10.7 Process Parameters

| Parameter | PVA glue (wood–wood) | Epoxy resin (wood–fibre) |
| :--- | :---------------: | :-----------------: |
| Optimal glue-spread amount | 0.08–0.10 g/cm² | 0.08–0.12 g/cm² |
| Recommended pressing pressure | 2.5–5 kPa | 5–10 kPa |
| Minimum curing time | ≥6 h | ≥24 h (23 °C) |
| Working window | Open time 10–12 min | Pot life 20–25 min |

### 10.8 Comprehensive Recommendations and Next Steps

1. **Scheme 1 (3-ply all-wood)**: the measured data confirm that the blade weight can be as low as 37–45 g, suitable for prototype validation of the bonding process.
2. **Scheme 2 (5-ply all-wood)**: recommended as the preferred all-wood scheme. The Kiri medial ply provides the necessary stiffness transition; the blade weighs 44–51 g.
3. **Scheme 3 (5+2 fibre composite)**: the highest-performance scheme. **ALC fibre + an outer structure** is recommended, significantly increasing rigidity through the fibre layers while retaining the lightweight advantage of the Balsa core.

**Recommendations for follow-up work**:

- Feed the measured density data back to [Three Blade Design Schemes and Procurement List](../%E9%98%B6%E6%AE%B52-%E8%B0%83%E7%A0%94%E4%BB%A5%E5%8F%8A%E6%9D%90%E6%96%99%E5%87%86%E5%A4%87/Three%20Blade%20Design%20Schemes%20and%20Procurement%20List.en.md) and correct the weight estimates of each scheme.
- According to the process parameters calibrated in Experiment 7, carry out prototype fabrication of the blades of Schemes 1/2/3.
- After prototyping is completed, evaluate the overall blade weight, centre-of-gravity position, and feel.
