# Research Report on the Density and Price of Lightweight Table Tennis Racket Materials

## 1 Introduction

Among the design objectives of the Octo table tennis racket, the total weight of the finished racket must be controlled within **110–130 g** (a 30 %–40 % weight reduction relative to a standard racket), of which the blade weight must be reduced to **55–70 g** (a 25 %–35 % weight reduction relative to a standard blade). The key to achieving this objective lies in selecting materials that are lightweight, high-performance, and economically obtainable.

This report systematically surveys the density, price, processing characteristics, and applicability of lightweight natural woods, synthetic fibre materials, and lightweight rubbers usable in table tennis blades, aiming to provide a quantitative basis for the material selection in Phase 2. The scope of the survey covers:

- The density, price, and applicable layer position of seven common blade woods
- The density, price, and performance characteristics of six synthetic fibre/blended materials
- The weight and price data of lightweight rubbers
- Exploration of special lightweight materials (such as cork and carbon foam)
- Comprehensive selection recommendations based on density–price–performance

> **Project-background note**: this project is maintained by high-school students; all scheme designs take easy obtainability and easy implementation as their primary principle, avoiding over-consideration of mass production or commercial application.

### 2.2 Individual Analysis of Lightweight Woods

#### 2.2.1 Balsa (Ochroma pyramidale)

- **Density**: 0.10–0.20 g/cm³ — the lightest of all commercial woods.
- **Price**: 3,000–8,000 CNY/m³. Small-format Balsa in hobby shops is more expensive; industrial-grade large panels (used for wind-turbine blades) can be obtained at a lower price through domestic suppliers such as Visight New Materials. Industrial-grade B100 density (100 kg/m³) panels cost about 60–80 CNY per piece (400×300×20 mm)[^4].
- **Characteristics**: extremely light and soft, uniform texture, easy to machine (a utility knife is enough to cut it), with excellent vibration-isolation performance. Excellent load-carrying capacity per unit weight under compression (high specific strength). Its natural decay resistance is poor and sealing treatment is required[^5].
- **Application cases in table tennis blades**:
  - JOOLA Eagle Fast (outer ply Fineline red + medial ply Sapele + core Balsa), weight 70–75 g
  - JOOLA Colt Off Speed (outer ply Limba + medial ply Makore + core Balsa), weight about 80 g
  - Tibhar Chila OFF (thick Balsa core), weight only 74.6 g
  - 729 Bomb (Balsa core + cork), weight about 85 g
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (extremely light, but must be combined with a hard outer ply or fibre to provide support)

#### 2.2.2 Kiri (Paulownia tomentosa)

- **Density**: 0.23–0.40 g/cm³ (Green Kiri at the denser end 0.30–0.40, common paulownia at the sparser end 0.23–0.30).
- **Price**: Grade-A Kiri laminated board 2,500–3,400 CNY/m³; instrument grade (knot-free, joint-free) up to 4,000–4,900 CNY/m³. The lowest price among lightweight woods[^6].
- **Characteristics**: straight grain, not easily deformed, moisture-resistant and damp-proof, good vibration absorption. Light and soft, but with insufficient support, so an assembled (edge-glued) structure is almost always used to increase strength. Green Kiri has more tightly packed fibres and clearly better support than common paulownia[^7].
- **Application cases in table tennis blades**:
  - Butterfly Viscaria (Kiri core), weight 86–92 g
  - Almost all Butterfly fibre blades made in Japan use a Kiri core
  - JOOLA Wing Medium (Koto + Ayous + Kiri), weight about 80 g
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (low density, low price, mature technology; the first choice for the core of a lightweight blade)

#### 2.2.3 Ayous (Triplochiton scleroxylon)

- **Density**: 0.29–0.45 g/cm³ (different sources give 0.29–0.33 or 0.35–0.45).
- **Price**: 2,000–4,000 CNY/m³ — one of the lowest prices among lightweight woods, with abundant supply (Gabon, West Africa).
- **Characteristics**: light yet tough, with excellent elasticity, known as "African whitewood". More than 90 % of table tennis rackets use Ayous for one of their layers. Its density is slightly higher than Kiri's, but its support and blade power are stronger[^8].
- **Application cases in table tennis blades**:
  - Stiga Offensive Classic (Ayous core), weight 82–88 g
  - Stiga Clipper Wood (5 of its 7 layers are Ayous), weight 90–95 g
  - JOOLA Falcon Medium (Ayous + Ayous + Tungwood), weight about 80 g
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (lightweight, good elasticity, low price, abundant blade power; the ideal core for an all-wood blade, but slightly heavier than Kiri)

#### 2.2.4 Hinoki (Chamaecyparis obtusa)

- **Density**: 0.35–0.50 g/cm³
- **Price**: 8,000–30,000 CNY/m³ — high, and rising continuously owing to increasingly scarce resources. Japanese Kiso Hinoki is even more extraordinarily expensive[^9].
- **Characteristics**: light, highly elastic, good toughness, straight and fine grain. Soft feel but fast ball-exit speed ("soft but springy"). A single-ply Hinoki blade (1-ply) is a classic choice for the Japanese penhold style.
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐ (excellent performance but too expensive; not suitable for a low-cost scheme; consider using only a thin layer for the outer ply to reduce usage)

#### 2.2.5 Spruce

- **Density**: 0.40–0.50 g/cm³
- **Price**: 3,000–6,000 CNY/m³
- **Characteristics**: uniform grain, good elasticity; as a medial ply it provides a "spring effect" (when installed laterally), increasing the overall deformation and spin capacity of the blade.
- **Typical application**: the medial ply of the Stiga OC.
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐ (moderately lightweight; a reasonable choice as a medial ply, but not a core weight-reduction material)

### 2.3 Priority of Lightweight Wood Selection

Based on a comprehensive assessment of density, price, and applicability:

| Priority | Wood | Recommended use | Reason |
| :---- | :--- | :------- | :--- |
| 1 | Kiri | Core | The mainstream wood with the lowest density, lowest price, mature technology, and excellent performance when combined with fibre |
| 2 | Ayous | Medial ply/core | Light and tough, extremely low price, good elasticity; suitable for all-wood schemes |
| 3 | Balsa | Core | Lowest density, but requires sealing treatment and has limited support; suitable for schemes pursuing extreme lightweighting |
| 4 | Hinoki | Outer ply | Excellent feel but expensive; can be used in a thin layer for the outer ply to control cost |
| 5 | Limba | Outer ply | Soft feel and good dwell, moderate price |
| 6 | Koto | Outer ply | Moderate hardness, crisp ball exit, low price; suitable as an attacking outer ply |

### 3.2 Detailed Analysis of Each Fibre Material

#### 3.2.1 Carbon Fibre

- **Areal density**: 60–80 g/m² (woven carbon), 40–60 g/m² (chopped carbon)
- **Estimated weight per layer**: 1.1–1.5 g (woven carbon, standard racket face)
- **Price**: 20–50 CNY/layer (domestic woven carbon); Butterfly T5000 carbon-fibre blades sell for 500–1,000 CNY as a whole
- **Characteristics**: extremely high strength, low specific gravity, small deformation under impact, fast recovery. It increases blade hardness and ball-exit speed and enlarges the sweet spot, but the feel is on the hard side and the dwell time is short[^13].
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐ (moderate weight, low price, but the feel is on the hard side and may not suit target users who need a soft feel)

#### 3.2.2 Aramid Fibre + ALC (Arylate / Arylate-Carbon)

- **Areal density**: 50–70 g/m² (ALC)
- **Estimated weight per layer**: 0.95–1.3 g
- **Price**: 50–120 CNY/layer (domestic ALC fibre cloth); genuine Butterfly ALC blades sell for 800–1,500 CNY
- **Characteristics**: aramid fibre has extremely high toughness, high viscoelasticity, and good vibration absorption. The ALC blend combines the flexibility of arylate and the rigidity of carbon, providing both dwell capability and ball-exit speed. Blue arylate-carbon is the most mainstream choice[^14].
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (balanced performance, soft feel, moderate price; the preferred fibre scheme for the Octo project)

#### 3.2.3 ZLF (Pure Zylon Fibre)

- **Areal density**: 35–50 g/m² — the lightest of all fibre materials
- **Estimated weight per layer**: 0.67–0.95 g
- **Price**: 150–300 CNY/layer (high material cost; Butterfly ZLF blades sell for 1,200–2,000 CNY)
- **Characteristics**: PBO fibre (poly-p-phenylene-2,6-benzobisoxazole), with more than twice the strength of aramid, an extremely soft feel, and the lightest weight. Butterfly calls it a "super fibre". ZLF is 10 %–15 % lighter than ALC, with a softer feel and abundant power[^15].
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐⭐ (the lightest fibre option with a soft feel, very suitable for lightweighting needs, but relatively expensive)

#### 3.2.4 ZLC (Zylon + Carbon Blend)

- **Areal density**: 40–60 g/m²
- **Estimated weight per layer**: 0.76–1.1 g
- **Price**: 200–400 CNY/layer (Butterfly ZLC blades sell for 1,500–2,800 CNY)
- **Characteristics**: a blend of about 50 % Zylon + 50 % carbon, 10 %–15 % lighter than ALC, with a softer feel while retaining the sweet spot and speed. It has the highest speed/control ratio[^16].
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐⭐ (excellent lightweighting effect, but the most expensive; suitable for high-performance needs)

#### 3.2.5 Glass Fibre

- **Areal density**: 60–90 g/m²
- **Estimated weight per layer**: 1.1–1.7 g
- **Price**: 10–30 CNY/layer — the cheapest fibre material
- **Characteristics**: moderate strength, soft feel, closest to all-wood. It can stabilise the feel and reduce individual variation[^17].
- **Applicability assessment**: ⭐⭐⭐ (the lowest cost, with a feel close to all-wood, but the weight-reduction effect is inferior to ZLF/ZLC)

### 4.4 Rubber-Selection Recommendations

- **Core strategy**: choose thin-sponge (1.5–1.8 mm) lightweight rubbers, giving preference to the 729 series (lowest cost) or the LKT series (outstanding lightweighting performance).
- **Price–performance balance**: 729 FX SuperSoft (~35 CNY/sheet) and LKT Pro XP (~40 CNY/sheet) are the most cost-effective lightweight choices.
- **Note**: rubbers that are too thin and too light may sacrifice ball-exit speed and spin ability; the feel feedback must be tested in actual prototyping.

## 5 Exploration of Special Lightweight Materials

### 5.1 Cork

- **Density**: 0.15–0.25 g/cm³
- **Price**: 5,000–15,000 CNY/m³ (depending on grade)
- **Use**: handle material, vibration-damping layer
- **Assessment**: the 729 Bomb blade uses cork as part of the core. Cork has excellent vibration-isolation performance and lightweight characteristics, making it suitable as a handle material to reduce the weight at the end and help shift the CG rearward.

### 5.2 Carbon Foam Sandwich

- **Density**: 0.20–0.40 g/cm³ (foam core)
- **Price**: high-end material with a relatively high cost
- **Use**: aerospace-grade composite structural material
- **Assessment**: it can provide extremely high specific strength and specific stiffness, but the fabrication difficulty is high, the cost is high, and the availability is poor, which does not conform to the "easy to obtain, easy to implement" principle. **Not recommended.**

### 5.3 Weight Reduction by Hollowing/Drilling the Wood

- **Method**: drill weight-reduction holes inside the core (similar to the design of a golf club head)
- **Weight-reduction effect**: can reduce weight by 5–15 g (depending on the hole diameter and number)
- **Price**: zero material cost, only machining cost
- **Assessment**: some niche blades use this method, but it may affect the sweet-spot size and structural integrity. **It can serve as an alternative scheme, but must be verified carefully.**

### 5.4 Kiri Splicing Optimisation

- **Method**: use a gradient splice of low-density Kiri (density ~0.25 g/cm³) and higher-density Kiri (~0.35 g/cm³)
- **Weight-reduction effect**: using lower-density Kiri on the racket-head side helps shift the CG rearward while reducing the overall weight by 3–5 g
- **Price**: zero additional material cost
- **Assessment**: highly feasible and compatible with the existing Kiri-core process. **It is recommended to consider a racket-head–handle density-gradient scheme in the design.**

### 6.3 Material-Selection Recommendations

Based on a comprehensive assessment of density, price, and performance, the following is recommended in order of priority:

**Core materials (Core)**:

1. **Kiri (Green Kiri)** — first choice. Low density (0.28–0.35 g/cm³), low price (2,500–3,400 CNY/m³), the most mature technology, and excellent performance when combined with fibre.
2. **Balsa** — alternative. Even lower density (0.10–0.20 g/cm³), but requires sealing treatment and its durability is questionable.
3. **Ayous** — alternative for all-wood schemes. Better elasticity but slightly higher density.

**Outer-ply materials (Outer Ply)**:

1. **Hinoki** (0.35–0.50 g/cm³) — soft feel and good elasticity, but expensive. A thin layer (0.4–0.5 mm) can be used to control cost.
2. **Limba** (0.40–0.55 g/cm³) — good dwell, moderate price.
3. **Koto** (0.55–0.65 g/cm³) — crisp ball exit, low price.

**Medial-ply materials (Intermediate Ply)**:

1. **Ayous** (0.29–0.45 g/cm³) — light and tough, good elasticity, low price.
2. **Spruce** (0.40–0.50 g/cm³) — a classic medial ply providing a spring effect.

**Fibre-layer materials**:

1. **ALC (arylate-carbon blend)** — balanced performance, soft feel, moderate price.
2. **ZLF (pure Zylon fibre)** — the lightest and softest feel, but the most expensive.
3. **ZLC (Zylon-carbon blend)** — lightweight and excellent performance, the most expensive.

**Rubber schemes**:

1. **Ultra-light**: 729 FX SuperSoft 1.5 mm (forehand) + LKT Pro XT 1.5 mm (backhand), total weight ~75 g.
2. **Economical balanced**: 729-40S 2.0 mm (forehand) + LKT Pro XP 1.8 mm (backhand), total weight ~83 g.

## 8 Feasibility Analysis of a Wood + Foam-Core Composite Structure

### 8.1 Types and Characteristics of Foam Core Materials

Foam core materials already have mature applications in the composite sandwich structures of ships, wind-turbine blades, skis, and surfboards. The following assesses their feasibility for use in table tennis blades.

#### 8.1.1 Comparison of Common Foam Core Materials

| Type | Density (kg/m³) | Characteristics | Application field |
| :--- | :----------: | :--- | :------- |
| PVC structural foam (closed-cell) | 45–250 | Good rigidity, closed-cell ratio >95 %, waterproof, compression resistance up to 6.2 MPa | Hulls, wind-turbine blades, skis |
| PET foam | 80–250 | Environmentally friendly and recyclable, good toughness | Wind-turbine blades, transport |
| PMI foam | 50–200 | Extremely high strength, high-temperature resistant | Aerospace |
| PU polyurethane foam | 30–200 | Low cost, good vibration isolation | Insulation, filling |
| Balsa (natural wood) | 100–200 | Extremely light, high specific strength, excellent vibration isolation | Wind-turbine blades, model aircraft, table tennis blades |

> **Table 11: Performance comparison of common foam core materials**[^21][^22]

### 8.2 Limitations of Using Foam Core Materials in Table Tennis Blades

#### 8.2.1 ITTF Regulatory Limitation (Key Constraint)

According to the ITTF rules: **"At least 85 % of the blade by thickness shall be of natural wood"**[^1]. This means:

- Foam material can only be classified within the "≤15 % synthetic material" quota.
- If the total blade thickness is 5.5 mm, the maximum permissible foam-core thickness is only **0.825 mm**.
- A 0.825 mm foam layer can hardly provide any meaningful weight reduction or vibration-isolation effect structurally.

**Conclusion: foam core material cannot be used as a main structural layer in a full-size blade.**

#### 8.2.2 Feasible Foam Application Scenarios

Although a full-size foam core is infeasible, the following local applications still have potential:

| Application position | Scheme description | Feasibility |
| :------- | :------- | :----- |
| **Handle core material** | Use foam material inside the handle to reduce weight and help shift the CG rearward | **Feasible** — Butterfly already has a patent (EP3560560) using resin foam to manufacture handle parts[^23] |
| **Ultra-thin damping interlayer** | Embed a 0.5 mm thin foam layer between the medial ply and the core | **Limited feasibility** — the bonding strength must be verified and the weight-reduction effect is limited |
| **Local core filling** | Drill out the core interior and fill it with lightweight foam | **Requires verification** — may affect the sweet spot and structural integrity |

### 8.3 Practical Cases of a Wood + Foam Composite Structure

#### 8.3.1 Existing Commercial Cases

- **729 Bomb blade**: uses a combination of a Balsa core + cork, weighing about 85 g — Balsa itself is a lightweight wood (natural wood) and does not belong to synthetic foam.
- **Donic Balsa Carbo series**: Balsa core (5 mm) + carbon fibre + Limba outer ply, weight 70–75 g — likewise uses natural Balsa rather than synthetic foam.
- **TSP Balsa Plus**: thick Balsa core (8.5 mm), extremely light but with very high speed — an all-Balsa + glass-fibre structure.

**Key observation**: the "lightweight core materials" used in the above cases are all Balsa (natural wood), not synthetic foam. In commercial promotion Balsa is often misnamed "foam wood" or "cork wood", but it is essentially a natural hardwood and fully complies with the ITTF 85 % wood requirement.

#### 8.3.2 Handmade DIY Cases

In overseas DIY communities (OOAK Forum, TableTennisDaily), enthusiasts have made handmade blades containing a Balsa core[^24]:

- **3+2 structure**: Balsa core (3/16" ≈ 4.8 mm) + mahogany outer ply + carbon fibre, weighing about 68 g.
- **Feedback**: the feel is good and the lightweighting effect is significant, but the Balsa core must be protected with hardwood edge banding.

#### 8.3.3 Application of Foam Core Materials in Other Racket Sports

- **Pickleball paddles**: widely use a PP honeycomb core + glass-fibre/carbon-fibre facing, weight 180–240 g.
- **Tennis rackets**: some models use foam filling in the frame for vibration damping.
- **Skis/surfboards**: widely use PVC/PET foam + glass-fibre/carbon-fibre sandwich structures.

None of the above applications is subject to a rule like the ITTF 85 % wood-proportion rule.

### 8.4 Comprehensive Feasibility Assessment

| Assessment dimension | Rating | Explanation |
| :------- | :--: | :--- |
| Weight-reduction effect | ★★★ | The density of synthetic foam can be as low as 45 kg/m³, but the actual weight-reduction contribution is very small, limited by the 15 % thickness restriction |
| ITTF compliance | ★☆☆☆☆ | Synthetic foam can hardly be used compliantly in the main structural layers of the blade |
| Fabrication difficulty | ★★★★ | PVC/PET foam can be precisely machined by CNC, but bonding to wood requires a special adhesive |
| Durability | ★★★ | Closed-cell foam is waterproof and decay-resistant, but the bonding interface with wood may delaminate under impact |
| Cost | ★★★★ | Structural foam costs 100–400 CNY/m², higher than Kiri but lower than ZLF fibre |
| Availability | ★★★ | Industrial foam is easy to buy (Taobao/1688), but thin specifications (<1 mm) are rare |
| Influence on feel | ★★★ | Foam significantly changes the hitting feel (more "muffled", more vibration-absorbing); experimental verification is required |
| Industry precedent | ★★ | There is almost no precedent for synthetic foam cores in commercial table tennis blades |

### 8.5 Conclusion

**A wood + synthetic foam-core composite structure has low feasibility in table tennis blades**, mainly for the following reasons:

1. **ITTF regulatory limitation**: the 85 % natural-wood rule means synthetic foam can only occupy a ≤15 % thickness quota, making the weight-reduction effect extremely limited.
2. **Industry practice**: almost all "lightweight sandwich" blades on the market use Balsa (natural wood) rather than synthetic foam.
3. **Bonding reliability**: the elastic-modulus difference between foam and wood is large, and long-term use may cause delamination.

**Better alternative schemes**:

- Use a **Balsa core** (density 0.10–0.20 g/cm³, natural wood, fully compliant) as a "natural foam" to replace synthetic foam.
- Use a **Kiri core + ALC/ZLF fibre** combination (the scheme recommended in Section 6).
- Consider using foam material only in the **handle region** to help shift the CG rearward.

## 9 Can a Balsa Blade Reach 60 g? — Quantitative Verification

### 9.1 Problem Definition

The user asked: "The blade needs to reach 60 g; can Balsa achieve this?" This section verifies it by quantitative calculation.

Here "blade" means the finished bare blade (excluding rubbers), including: the racket face (multiple wood layers + fibre layers + glue) + handle + surface coating.

### 9.2 Calculation Model

A **5+2 fibre composite structure** is adopted, with a standard racket-face area of ~190 cm²; each layer is calculated as follows:

#### 9.2.1 Racket-Face Part (excluding the handle)

| Component | Material | Thickness (mm) | Density (g/cm³) | Mass (g) | Formula |
| :--- | :--- | :-------: | :----------: | :------: | :----- |
| Outer ply × 2 | Koto | 0.50 | 0.60 | 11.40 | 0.05 × 190 × 0.60 × 2 |
| Fibre layer × 2 | ALC | 0.23 | — | 2.20 | Estimated weight (190 cm² × 58 g/m² × 2) |
| Medial ply × 2 | Ayous | 0.60 | 0.35 | 7.98 | 0.06 × 190 × 0.35 × 2 |
| Core × 1 | **Balsa** | **t** | **ρ_balsa** | **190 × t × ρ_balsa** | See the variables below |
| Glue | PVA/epoxy | — | — | 3.00 | Estimate for 6 layers |
| **Racket-face subtotal** | | | | **24.58 + 190 × t × ρ_balsa** | |

#### 9.2.2 Handle + Coating

| Component | Mass (g) |
| :--- | :------: |
| Handle (shortened to 70 mm, fitting the hands of patients with dwarfism) | 12–15 |
| Surface coating (2–3 coats of polyurethane/varnish) | 2–3 |
| **Subtotal** | **14–18** |

#### 9.2.3 Blade Total-Weight Formula

$$W_{\text{blade}} = 24.58 + 190 \times t_{\text{core}} \times \rho_{\text{Balsa}} + 3.0_{\text{glue}} + (14\text{–}18)_{\text{handle+coating}}$$

Taking the simplified median value (handle + coating = 16 g):

$$W_{\text{blade}} \approx 40.6 + 190 \times t_{\text{core}} \times \rho_{\text{Balsa}}$$

| 3.0 mm | 5.70 g | 33.3 g | 16 g | **49.3 g** |
| 4.0 mm | 7.60 g | 35.2 g | 16 g | **51.2 g** |
| 5.0 mm | 9.50 g | 37.1 g | 16 g | **53.1 g** |

**Conclusion**: even at a thickness of 5 mm, low-density Balsa gives a total blade weight of only **~53 g**, below the 60 g target.

| **5.0 mm** | **14.25 g** | **41.9 g** | **16 g** | **57.9 g** |

**Conclusion**: medium-density Balsa + a 5 mm core gives a total blade weight of **~58 g**, very close to the 60 g target.

### 9.5 Methods of Increasing the Blade Weight to 60 g

If low-density Balsa (0.10–0.15 g/cm³) is used, the calculation shows that the blade will be too light (47–58 g); the weight can be increased to 60 g by the following methods:

| Method | Weight increase | Advantages and disadvantages |
| :--- | :------: | :----- |
| **Increase the core thickness** | +2–6 g/mm | Makes the blade thicker and harder and enlarges the sweet spot; above 6 mm the feel may be too thick |
| **Use a heavier outer ply** (e.g. Koto 0.60 → Ebony 0.95) | +2–4 g | Increases hardness and ball-exit speed, but reduces the dwell feel |
| **Thicken the outer ply/medial ply** | +1–3 g | Improves overall rigidity, but increases cost |
| **Increase the number of fibre layers** (1 layer → 2 layers of ALC) | +1–2 g | Increases rigidity and the sweet spot |
| **Use a heavier handle material** (e.g. switch to a hardwood handle) | +2–5 g | Helps shift the CG rearward and does not affect the racket-face feel |
| **Increase the internal counterweight in the handle** | +1–3 g | Allows free adjustment of the CG position |

**Recommended weight-increase strategy to 60 g**:

1. When low-density Balsa (0.10–0.15 g/cm³) is used, choose a **hardwood handle** (e.g. Wenge or ebony), raising the handle weight from 12 g to 16–18 g.
2. At the same time, increase the core thickness to **5 mm** to increase the racket-face weight.

The weights of the above commercial blades are all higher than 60 g (70–75 g), for the following reasons:

- The handle length and weight are moderate (a standard handle is 18–22 g, whereas in this project it can be shortened to below 15 g).
- The outer ply/medial ply are relatively thick, and heavier hardwood may be used.
- Some contain multiple fibre layers.

Therefore, by reducing the handle size and using the lightest outer-ply/medial-ply configuration, **it is entirely feasible to reduce a commercial 70 g Balsa blade to 60 g**.

### 9.7 Conclusion

| Question | Answer |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Can a Balsa blade reach 60 g?** | **✅ Yes** |
| Optimal configuration | Medium-density Balsa (0.15–0.20 g/cm³) + 4–5 mm core + light outer ply + a moderately weighted handle |
| Expected blade weight | **55–63 g** (can be fine-tuned precisely to 60 g with a handle counterweight) |
| Is there a safety margin? | **Yes** — even with the lightest Balsa (0.10 g/cm³), it can be adjusted to 60 g by thickening the core or weighting the handle |
| Weight-reduction advantage over the Kiri scheme | The Balsa scheme can reduce weight by a further **5–10 g** compared with the Kiri scheme (blade 51–60 g) |
| Disadvantages to note | Balsa is extremely soft and requires hardwood edge banding for protection; the feel is on the "muffled" side (strong vibration absorption); the impact performance is non-linear (light touches are controllable, heavy hits spring noticeably) |

**Final recommendation**: if the budget allows and one does not mind the "soft-springy" feel characteristic of Balsa, using a Balsa core with a thickness of 4–5 mm allows the blade weight to be flexibly adjusted within the 55–63 g range, fully able to achieve the 60 g target.

## 10 Conclusions and Recommendations

### 10.1 Core Conclusions

1. **Core-material selection**: Kiri (Green Kiri), because of its low density (0.28–0.35 g/cm³), low price (2,500–3,400 CNY/m³), and mature technology, is the **optimal choice** for the core of the Octo lightweight blade. Balsa can serve as an alternative for pursuing extreme lightweighting.
2. **Fibre-material selection**: ALC (arylate-carbon blend) achieves the best balance among performance, price, and obtainability, and is the **recommended preferred fibre scheme**. If the budget allows and one pursues extreme lightweighting and a soft feel, ZLF fibre may be chosen.
3. **Rubber weight reduction**: by choosing lightweight rubbers such as 729 FX SuperSoft or LKT Pro XP (1.5–1.8 mm sponge), the weight per side of rubber can be reduced to 36–42 g (versus the standard 46–53 g), giving a reduction of 15–25 g across both sides.
4. **Achievability of the total weight**: with a configuration of Kiri core + ALC fibre + lightweight rubbers, the total weight of the finished racket can be controlled within the **130–148 g** range. If a Balsa core + ultra-light rubbers are further used, **123 g** is expected, approaching the lower limit of the 110–130 g target.
5. **Cost control**: the material cost of the economical scheme is about 120 CNY/blade and that of the advanced scheme about 220 CNY/blade, both within the tolerable range of a high-school student project.

### 10.2 Recommended Schemes

Taking into comprehensive account the **lightweighting effect, cost, obtainability, and fabrication difficulty**, the following two schemes are recommended as the material basis for the Octo table tennis racket:

**Primary recommendation — Kiri core + ALC fibre scheme:**

- Core: Green Kiri (density ~0.30 g/cm³)
- Medial ply: Ayous (density ~0.35 g/cm³)
- Outer ply: a thin Hinoki layer (0.4 mm) or Koto (0.5 mm)
- Fibre: ALC (blue arylate-carbon blend)
- Rubbers: 729-40S (forehand) + LKT Pro XP (backhand)
- **Estimated blade weight: ~55–60 g; total finished weight: ~130–140 g**

**Exploratory alternative — Balsa-core ultra-light scheme:**

- Core: Balsa (density ~0.15 g/cm³), requiring sealing treatment
- Medial ply: a thin Ayous layer
- Outer ply: Hinoki (0.4 mm)
- Fibre: chopped carbon or glass fibre (support reinforcement)
- Rubbers: 729 FX SuperSoft 1.5 mm (both sides)
- **Estimated blade weight: ~48–52 g; total finished weight: ~120–125 g**
